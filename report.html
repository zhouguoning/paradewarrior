Task 5: value every flat, with a repeat-sales ladder that knows which building you're in

We valued all 181,711 City of Sydney addresses as at 31 August 2026. On 421 later flat sales we held out (1 June to 31 August 2026, under $1.2M), 53.9% of our estimates landed within 10% of the sale price. On the same flats where the task page's best baseline applies (last sale updated by a citywide index), that baseline scored 48.3% and we scored 56.0%, with typical dollar error down from $90,250 to $64,062.

Flats are a slow market. The index shows unit prices in the City of Sydney have barely moved in real terms over the last decade, so a flat's own last sale, even an old one, is a strong anchor once it's moved forward by the index.

Suburbs drift apart from the city. We measured each suburb's annual growth above or below the citywide index over the last four years, shrunk toward zero where evidence is thin. Biggest gaps, % a year versus the city:

Stacked units share layouts. 73% of addresses have no bedroom count. But Unit 1204 usually sits directly above Unit 1104 with the same floor plan, so we borrowed bedrooms from the same position on other floors of the same building. That raised by about 45% the number of flats we can match to a same-building, same-bedroom comparable.

Height pays, a little. Within the same building and bedroom count, each extra floor added about 0.5% to the sale price, so floor 15 versus floor 2 is worth roughly 7%. We read the floor straight out of the unit number (Unit 1204 is on floor 12).

The scoring window is selective. Only sales under $1.2M are scored. On our test our estimates ran a median 5.0% high, part of which is this truncation: expensive flats that happen to sell cheaply stay in, cheap flats that sell dear drop out.

We picked every tuning parameter on two earlier validation folds (December 2025 to February 2026, and March to May 2026; average 57.5% within 10% over 945 sales). Only then did we rebuild on sales before 1 June 2026 and score June to August once. No test-period price was used to build or tune anything. All 421 test flats received a prediction.

R² is low because a handful of large misses dominate a squared-error metric; the judged metric (within 10%) is about the typical flat, where we do well.

Data

  • flat-sales-city-of-sydney.csv: 81,078 sales from 1995 to 31 August 2026 (Microburbs, supplied for the day; not republished).
  • flats-city-of-sydney.csv: the 181,711 target addresses with unit number, building size and last-advert bedrooms.
  • We built our own repeat-sales pairs from the sales file (it runs to 31 August, the supplied pair file stops in April 2026).
  • Addresses: Open G-NAF © Geoscape Australia. Nothing dated after 31 August 2026 was used.

Method

  1. Repeat-sales index. Quarterly Case-Shiller-style regression of log price change on time dummies, weighted down for long holds, smoothed with a second-difference penalty, outliers trimmed at 4 MAD.
  2. Local drift. Each suburb's and building's growth above or below the city, as % per year, shrunk toward zero (Bayesian-style, by holding years).
  3. Value ladder. Every past sale is moved to 31 August 2026. For each flat we take the best evidence available: same building and bedrooms → same building (lower quantile when bedrooms unknown) → suburb and bedrooms → suburb → city.
  4. Floor and thin cells. Building comparables are moved to the target flat's floor, and building-and-bedroom groups with few sales are shrunk toward the suburb-and-bedroom median.
  5. Blend. If the flat sold before, blend its own index-adjusted sale with the building comparable in log space, trusting old sales less (weight halves after 5 years).

What did not work, and what remains uncertain

  • Flats with an old sale but no known bedrooms are weak (few test cases, large misses): the building may mix studios and 3-bedders.
  • A global downward shift for the $1.2M truncation did not help on validation, so we left it out rather than tune on the test.
  • Tuning on two folds raised validation accuracy from 55.9% to 57.5%, but on the final test it scored 53.9% against 54.9% for our simpler first version. With 421 test sales, one percentage point is about four flats, well inside the noise, so we kept the validation-chosen settings rather than pick by test score. Like-for-like against the baseline it improved (56.0% vs 53.4% for v1), and R² rose from 0.12 to 0.20.
  • About 6,700 shop and office titles get a value but are probably poorly valued; they are not scored.
  • Not yet used: aspect and views. Both need outside data (building footprints and orientation) and a new dated check.

Task 4: what will this flat rent for?

We estimated two weekly asking rents, with and without a car space, for all 65,777 flats as at 31 July 2026 (rents_paradewarrior.csv). On 2,592 later adverts we held out (1 June to 31 July 2026), our typical miss was $44 a week and 77.7% landed within $100. A suburb-and-bedrooms median on the same adverts missed by $94 (51.4% within $100). The task page reports $100 for its simple estimate and $86 for the Microburbs rental valuation on the judging set.

Parking is worth most to one-bedders. Comparing adverts in the same building with the same bedroom count, a car space added (%):

Studios rarely come with parking, and three-bedders usually do, so a car space separates one-bedders more than anyone else. Comparing within the building strips out location and building quality, which a suburb-wide comparison would wrongly credit to parking.

Rents keep moving after the cutoff. Rents rose about 0.8% a quarter over the last year. Carrying half a quarter of that trend forward to the August to September scoring window was the single biggest improvement in our check. The task page notes the Microburbs valuation ran $61 a week low, which is consistent with this lag.

The flat's own last advert is the best clue. 75% of flats had been advertised before. Moved forward with our repeat-rent index and blended with same-building, same-bedroom adverts, those flats are where we are most accurate.

Data and method

  • rents-past-leases-city-of-sydney.csv: 112,457 adverts from 2012 to 31 July 2026, and rents-input-flats-city-of-sydney.csv (Microburbs, supplied for the day; not republished). Nothing after 31 July 2026 was used, and no live listings.
  • Repeat-rent index: quarterly regression on 54,473 pairs of adverts for the same flat with the same parking status, smoothed and outlier-trimmed.
  • Parking normalisation: every advert is converted to its "no car space" equivalent using the within-building premium for its bedroom band, so flats with and without parking can be compared directly. The with-parking column adds the premium back.
  • Ladder: own last advert (time-adjusted, trusted less as it ages) blended with same-building, same-bedroom adverts (shrunk toward the suburb when the building has few), then suburb × bedrooms, suburb, bedrooms, city. All 65,777 flats get both rents, including those outside the City block file.

What remains uncertain

  • Our check covers June to July; the judges score August to September, which is spring leasing season. We tuned on last year's August to September to cover this.
  • A floor premium (about 0.3% a floor) did not improve rents on validation, so it is off, unlike in our sale-price model.
  • Flats never advertised before (about a quarter) rely on building and suburb comparables and are less accurate.
