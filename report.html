Value every flat: a repeat-sales ladder that knows which building you're in

We valued all 181,711 City of Sydney addresses as at 31 August 2026. On 421 later flat sales we held out (1 June to 31 August 2026, under $1.2M), 54.9% of our estimates landed within 10% of the sale price. On the same flats where the task page's best baseline applies (last sale updated by a citywide index), that baseline scored 48.3% and we scored 53.4%, with typical dollar error down from $90,250 to $63,541.

Flats are a slow market. The index shows unit prices in the City of Sydney have barely moved in real terms over the last decade, so a flat's own last sale, even an old one, is a strong anchor once it's moved forward by the index.

Suburbs drift apart from the city. We measured each suburb's annual growth above or below the citywide index over the last four years, shrunk toward zero where evidence is thin. Biggest gaps, % a year versus the city:

Stacked units share layouts. 73% of addresses have no bedroom count. But Unit 1204 usually sits directly above Unit 1104 with the same floor plan, so we borrowed bedrooms from the same position on other floors of the same building. That raised by about 45% the number of flats we can match to a same-building, same-bedroom comparable.

The scoring window is selective. Only sales under $1.2M are scored. On our test our estimates ran a median 4.6% high, part of which is this truncation: expensive flats that happen to sell cheaply stay in, cheap flats that sell dear drop out.

What our held-out check showed

We picked every tuning parameter on a separate validation fold (built on sales before 1 March 2026, tested on March to May: 58.9% within 10%, R² 0.48). Only then did we rebuild on sales before 1 June 2026 and score June to August once. No test-period price was used to build or tune anything. All 421 test flats received a prediction.

R² is low because a handful of large misses dominate a squared-error metric; the judged metric (within 10%) is about the typical flat, where we do well.

Data

  • flat-sales-city-of-sydney.csv: 81,078 sales from 1995 to 31 August 2026 (Microburbs, supplied for the day; not republished).
  • flats-city-of-sydney.csv: the 181,711 target addresses with unit number, building size and last-advert bedrooms.
  • We built our own repeat-sales pairs from the sales file (it runs to 31 August, the supplied pair file stops in April 2026).
  • Addresses: Open G-NAF © Geoscape Australia. Nothing dated after 31 August 2026 was used.

Method

  1. Repeat-sales index. Quarterly Case-Shiller-style regression of log price change on time dummies, weighted down for long holds, smoothed with a second-difference penalty, outliers trimmed at 4 MAD.
  2. Local drift. Each suburb's and building's growth above or below the city, as % per year, shrunk toward zero (Bayesian-style, by holding years).
  3. Value ladder. Every past sale is moved to 31 August 2026. For each flat we take the best evidence available: same building and bedrooms → same building (lower quantile when bedrooms unknown) → suburb and bedrooms → suburb → city.
  4. Blend. If the flat sold before, blend its own index-adjusted sale with the building comparable in log space, trusting old sales less (weight halves after 5 years).

What did not work, and what remains uncertain

  • Flats with an old sale but no known bedrooms are weak (few test cases, large misses): the building may mix studios and 3-bedders.
  • A global downward shift for the $1.2M truncation did not help on validation, so we left it out rather than tune on the test.
  • About 6,700 shop and office titles get a value but are probably poorly valued; they are not scored.
  • Not yet used: floor level, aspect, views. Floor is recoverable from unit numbers (Unit 1204 → floor 12) and is the obvious next step.
